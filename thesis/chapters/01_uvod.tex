\section{Introduction}

\subsection{Motivation and research context}

Over the past decade, Bitcoin has gone from a niche technological experiment to an asset class held and monitored by institutional investors. This shift has direct implications for risk management: once a previously isolated market becomes large enough to attract significant capital, its price movements begin to affect portfolios that hold no cryptocurrency directly. For risk management the important part is the time variation of this influence: how and when the dependence between Bitcoin and conventional markets changes.

This thesis focuses on \emph{time-varying intermarket dependency}: the evolving statistical relationship between Bitcoin and conventional assets such as equity indices, gold, silver, and the dollar. The question is whether this correlation is predictable at all, and whether cryptocurrency price dynamics contain an early warning signal about stress in traditional markets. Its average level is less important here than the way it moves.

The practical motivation is specific. A risk manager does not need an exact point forecast of returns to benefit from this kind of analysis. What matters is the ability to recognise when co-movement patterns change: when the market regime shifts from decorrelated to tightly coupled, or when the probability of a stress day in conventional markets rises. Even a modest and statistically reliable signal of this type can be useful, if its limitations are understood.

\subsection{Research questions}

The thesis is built on two interrelated layers. The first is a forecasting problem: can the rolling correlation between Bitcoin and a conventional asset be predicted one step ahead, and how do machine-learning models compare with a classical econometric benchmark under strict out-of-sample evaluation? The second is an applied question: can these forecasts be turned into a practical warning signal for investors?

More specifically, four research questions are addressed:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Is the rolling dependency between Bitcoin and conventional assets forecastable out of sample?
    \item Do machine-learning models outperform a leakage-safe DCC-GARCH benchmark?
    \item Do machine-learning models add value beyond simple persistence-based baselines such as AR(1)?
    \item Can crypto-derived dependency forecasts support a practically useful stress-warning signal for conventional markets?
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Contributions of the thesis}

To address these questions, a reproducible Python pipeline was created from scratch. It loads price data, builds log-return series, computes rolling correlations across multiple window lengths, constructs lagged and volatility-based features, and evaluates a range of models within a walk-forward experimental design. The model set includes a naive random-walk baseline and an AR(1) autoregressive model, a Heterogeneous AutoRegressive (HAR) model that uses daily, weekly, and monthly dependency lags, regularised linear models (Ridge, ElasticNet), an adaptive inverse-RMSE-weighted ensemble, tree-based ensembles (Random Forest, GBM, XGBoost), and a leakage-safe DCC-GARCH(1,1) benchmark. On top of the forecasting stage, a second layer converts the forecasts into a binary stress classifier for conventional assets.

The main empirical conclusion is that the dependency process can be predicted out of sample, but much of this predictability comes from the target's own past; new cross-asset information does not add much. Ridge, AR(1), and HAR end up at the top with almost the same accuracy, because all three rely on the high serial persistence of the rolling correlation. The convergence of three structurally different models is eloquent in itself: the target has an almost linear autoregressive structure, and additional model complexity buys almost nothing. Machine learning nonetheless adds clear value over DCC-GARCH, and it is most useful in the stress-classification task, where regularised linear classifiers outperform tree ensembles.

Three specific contributions follow from this work. First, a reproducible Python pipeline makes the complete empirical record replicable from the raw data to the final figures. Second, a systematic comparison of ten models, ranging from naive persistence to XGBoost and DCC-GARCH within a consistent walk-forward design, quantifies where machine learning adds value and where it does not. Third, an explicit mapping from rolling-correlation forecasts to a binary stress classifier translates the statistical results into a form usable by a risk practitioner.
