\section{Conclusion}

This thesis dealt with a specific question: whether information about the evolving relationship between Bitcoin and conventional assets helps to predict that relationship, and whether the obtained forecasts can be made useful for risk management. The response to both is affirmative, with important clarifications as to what ``useful'' means.

The dependency process can be predicted out of sample. This result is valid for six asset pairs, four rolling-window lengths, and several families of models evaluated under a strict walk-forward protocol. Longer windows provide a smoother and more autocorrelated target and, accordingly, a higher out-of-sample $R^2$: at the 90-day window even the simplest AR(1) model reaches an $R^2$ between 0.982 and 0.992 across all six pairs.

This observation is also a major limitation: the prediction problem is largely solved by persistence. Since a 30-day rolling correlation shares 29 of its 30 observations with the previous day's value, it is difficult to improve on a forecast that sets tomorrow's correlation equal to today's. Ridge, AR(1), and HAR end up within the range of statistical noise, with the naive last-value baseline immediately behind them; Random Forest, GBM, and XGBoost perform substantially worse. The tree-based methods do not overcome the autoregressive baseline, whereas Ridge matches it, and every machine-learning model is superior to the DCC-GARCH benchmark. This last comparison should be read with caution: the target is a smoothed rolling-Pearson correlation, whereas DCC-GARCH estimates the instantaneous conditional correlation, so projecting the latter onto the former leads to a systematic lag that works against the benchmark. The relevant interpretation is narrow: the flexible data-driven methods track this particular smoothed target more accurately than the parametric alternative, although they do not exceed simple persistence. This is not a general conclusion that DCC-GARCH is an inferior model of co-movement.

The feature-group ablation of Section~\ref{sec:ablation} makes the same point from the other direction. A single dependency lag already carries most of the forecastable variation, the history of the dependency series adds the only real improvement, and the cross-asset blocks add nothing for the linear model and actively harm the tree ensembles, which degrade monotonically as further groups are supplied.

The investor signal layer is more nuanced. Balanced accuracy varies from below 0.50 to about 0.53, and AUC from roughly 0.48 to 0.56. These are modest values that lie above chance for most, but not all, configurations; the Bitcoin--NASDAQ pair falls below chance at the longer windows. Signal quality is highest for the Bitcoin--S\&P~500 pair, where the cryptocurrency feature set corresponds most closely to the macro-financial forces that drive broad equity stress. For gold, silver, and the dollar index the signal is weaker, which is consistent with those markets responding to drivers that Bitcoin dynamics capture only weakly.

From a practical point of view, the signal should enter a risk-management process as one input among several; treated as a standalone alarm, it would be oversold. It does not predict next-day returns, but it provides a small and consistent shift towards caution when the dependency structure is changing, and this is a justified use case.

Future work should test the framework at longer forecast horizons, where the advantage of persistence may decay more quickly; with richer feature sets, including on-chain metrics and macro-financial variables; and against tail-dependence measures that capture joint extreme behaviour more precisely than rolling Pearson correlation. A portfolio backtest with explicit transaction costs would help to reduce the gap between statistical and economic performance. These extensions are reserved for later work, for which the present thesis provides the foundation.
